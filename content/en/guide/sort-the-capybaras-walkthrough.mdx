## What Sort the Capybaras Actually Asks You to Do

Strip away the artwork and every sorting puzzle is a resource-management problem. Capybaras of different colors, patterns, or accessories sit in containers — tubes, shelves, baskets, or pools, depending on the version you play. Your job is to regroup them so every capybara shares a container with its own kind.

The twist is that you can usually only move the topmost capybara in a stack, and you can only place it somewhere empty or onto a matching capybara. That single restriction turns a five-second task into a twenty-move puzzle. Every move either creates breathing room or burns it, and the board remembers.

Most builds also add pressure through locked capybaras, limited container space, or a move counter. In player experience, the hardest boards are rarely the ones with the most capybaras. They are the ones with the fewest free slots.

## Core Mechanics at a Glance

Before you plan a single move, make sure you understand what each element on the board is doing. The table below covers the pieces you will see in nearly every version.

| Element | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Capybara units | Individual animals, each defined by a color, pattern, or accessory | Matching is decided by the trait, not the position |
| Containers | Tubes, shelves, baskets, or pools that hold a stack | Only the top unit of a stack can move |
| Empty slots | Free space inside a container | Your only real currency on the board |
| Locked units | Capybaras that cannot move until a condition is met | They quietly shrink your working space |
| Move counter or timer | Optional limit in some modes | Rewards planning over trial and error |
| Undo button | Reverses your last action | The single most underused tool in the game |

### The three rules that never change

- A capybara can only move if it is on top of its stack.
- A capybara can only land in an empty container or on a matching capybara.
- Containers have a fixed capacity, so you can never stack beyond the limit.

If you internalize those three rules, you already understand 90 percent of any sorting puzzle. The remaining 10 percent is the order in which you apply them.

## Sort the Capybaras Walkthrough: The Universal Five-Step Method

This is the part most players skip, and it is the part that saves the most moves. Instead of tapping the first capybara that looks out of place, run the board through the following sequence every time you start a new level.

| Step | Action | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scan the entire board before touching anything | You cannot plan around pieces you have not seen |
| 2 | Count how many distinct capybara groups exist | It tells you how many containers you ultimately need |
| 3 | Identify locked or blocked units first | These are the bottlenecks that decide the whole level |
| 4 | Start with the most constrained container | The tightest stack limits your options the most |
| 5 | Reverse-plan the final state, then work backward | Knowing the end position removes guesswork |

### Step 1: Scan before you sort

Beginners tap immediately. Experienced players spend five to ten seconds reading the board. Look for containers that are already almost complete — those are free wins — and containers that are completely mixed, which are the ones that will demand your attention later.

### Step 2: Count your groups

If you have four capybara types and five containers, you have one spare slot of working room. That is tight. If you have four types and six containers, you have real flexibility and can afford a few exploratory moves. Knowing the ratio before you start changes how aggressively you should play.

### Step 3: Handle locked capybaras early

Locked units are the most common reason players get stuck late in a level. Community reports suggest that clearing a lock two or three moves earlier than feels natural often saves a dozen moves later. If a lock is sitting under a mismatched stack, that stack becomes your top priority.

### Step 4: Attack the tightest container

A container with one empty slot has exactly one move available. A container with three empty slots has many. Always relieve the tight one first, because it is the piece of the board most likely to trap you.

### Step 5: Work backward from the finish

Picture the completed board: every capybara grouped, every container tidy. Now ask which container must be cleared first to make that picture possible. Reverse planning is the single biggest difference between players who finish a level in one attempt and players who restart four times.

## Early, Mid, and Late Game: How the Difficulty Curve Works

The same five-step method applies throughout, but the emphasis shifts as boards get denser.

| Stage | Typical board shape | What to focus on |
|---|---|---|
| Early levels | Few groups, generous empty space | Building good habits and learning to scan |
| Mid-game | More groups, tighter containers | Protecting empty slots and clearing locks early |
| Late levels | Dense stacks, multiple locks, move limits | Reverse planning and disciplined undos |

### Early levels: build the habit

Early boards are forgiving, which makes them the perfect place to practice reading the whole board before you move. If you train yourself to scan first now, you will not have to unlearn panic-tapping later.

### Mid-game: manage scarcity

This is where empty slots start to feel precious. A useful mental rule: never spend your last empty slot unless the move clears a container or frees a lock. Anything else is a loan you will pay back with interest.

### Late levels: plan in reverse

On dense boards, the winning move is often the one that looks wasteful at first glance. If a move consolidates two matching capybaras and frees a slot, take it even if it does not immediately complete a stack.

## Common Mistakes That Cost You the Level

Most failures are not caused by bad luck. They are caused by four repeatable errors.

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Hoarding empty slots | Refusing to use free space at all | Spend a slot when it clears a container or a lock |
| Ignoring locks | Sorting visible stacks while locks pile up | Treat locked units as your first priority |
| Moving without a target | Shuffling capybaras to "see what happens" | Name the outcome of a move before you make it |
| Refusing to undo | Pushing forward after two wasted moves | Undo immediately and re-plan from the clean state |

There is also a subtler error: treating every container as equally important. They are not. The container with the least free space controls your entire level, and it should dictate your move order until it is resolved.

## Boosters, Undos, and When to Spend Them

Boosters feel like a safety net, but using them at the wrong moment wastes them. Here is a simple framework for deciding.

| Tool | Best used when | Save it when |
|---|---|---|
| Undo | You have made two moves that created no progress | You are still exploring a fresh board |
| Extra container | A board has more capybara groups than containers | You still have unused empty slots |
| Shuffle | The board is fully locked with no legal progress | You can still free a lock manually |
| Hint | You have scanned the board twice and see nothing | You have not finished your initial scan |

The general principle: boosters should convert a losing position into a winnable one, not rescue a position you could have fixed with two thoughtful moves.

## How to Get Faster and Score Higher

Speed in sorting puzzles is not about tapping quickly. It is about eliminating wasted moves. Players who finish levels in the fewest moves almost always pause longer before their first tap.

A few habits that consistently help:

- Say the destination out loud in your head before every move.
- Batch matching moves — if three capybaras of the same type are exposed, place them in one continuous sequence.
- Finish one container completely rather than working on three at once.
- After clearing a level, replay it once to see if you can beat your move count.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is there an official sort the capybaras walkthrough with exact answers for every level?**

Not really. Board layouts can vary between versions and updates, so a fixed answer list goes stale quickly. What stays useful is a method: scan the board, count your groups, clear locks early, relieve the tightest container, and plan backward from the finished state. That approach works on any layout.

**Why do I keep running out of empty slots?**

You are probably spending free space on moves that do not clear anything. Player experience suggests the fix is simple: only use an empty slot when the move either completes a container or frees a locked capybara. Everything else is a temporary shuffle.

**Should I use boosters on hard levels or save them?**

Save them for boards where you have scanned twice and genuinely see no legal path forward. Using a shuffle or extra container on a board you could still solve manually just moves the problem to the next level.

**Does the game get harder over time?**

In most versions, yes — boards get denser, more capybara groups appear, and locked units become more common. The good news is that the underlying logic never changes, so the habits you build early keep paying off as the puzzles get tighter.

Master the five-step method, respect your empty slots, and treat locked capybaras as the priority they are. Do that, and you will stop restarting levels and start clearing them on the first attempt.
