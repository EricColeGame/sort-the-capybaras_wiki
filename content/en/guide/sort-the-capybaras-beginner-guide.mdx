## The Core Rules in Plain English

Every version of this puzzle runs on the same five mechanics. Learn them once and you can pick up any capybara sorter in under a minute.

| Element | What it does | Beginner takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Capybara tokens | Individual capybaras, each with a color, outfit, or accessory | The pattern, not the cuteness, is the information |
| Stacks / rows / pens | Hold capybaras in a fixed order | Only the front-most capybara is ever movable |
| Empty slots | Act as temporary parking spaces | Your most valuable and most misused resource |
| A legal move | Move a capybara onto a matching capybara, or into an empty slot | Matching moves are free; empty-slot moves cost you flexibility |
| Win condition | Every stack contains only one kind of capybara | You finish by consolidating, not by shuffling |

Two extra mechanics show up in most builds and change how you should play. The first is an **undo button**, sometimes limited per level — it's a safety net, not a strategy. The second is a **preview or hint system**, which is best used to confirm a plan you already have rather than to discover one.

The capybara theme isn't just decoration, by the way. The animals are famously social and calm — the [real-world capybara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara) lives in groups, which is a fitting mascot for a game about gathering your herd into the right pen.

### The rule new players forget

A stack has an order. If a red capybara sits behind a blue one, you cannot touch it until the blue one leaves. Every "impossible" board is usually just one buried capybara that nobody accounted for.

## Your First Board: A Six-Step Walkthrough

Here's the exact sequence to run on any new level, from the tutorial to the late game.

| Step | Action | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scan the whole board before tapping anything | You need a full inventory before you spend a move |
| 2 | Count how many capybaras of each type exist | Tells you which stacks are already correct and which are split |
| 3 | Identify the most buried type | The hardest capybara to free dictates the whole solve |
| 4 | Solve that buried type first | Late-game freedom comes from clearing constraints early |
| 5 | Consolidate matching stacks in batches | Reduces the number of "open" piles you're tracking |
| 6 | Spend an empty slot only when no matching move exists | Preserves flexibility for the endgame |

Steps 1 and 2 take about ten seconds and prevent the majority of beginner losses. Most players tap the first obvious match they see, which is the puzzle equivalent of paying the first bill in the pile instead of the largest one.

Step 4 is the one that separates smooth solves from ugly ones. Think of the board as a set of locks: a capybara buried under four others is a lock with four keys. Freeing it early means the rest of the board gets easier with every move instead of harder.

## Move Priority: The One Skill That Fixes Most Boards

If you only take one thing from this sort the capybaras beginner guide, take this ranking. When you're unsure what to do, work down the list.

| Priority | Move type | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Match onto a stack that is already mostly one type | Always — this is progress |
| 2 | Move a capybara that unblocks a buried one | Whenever the target stack has room |
| 3 | Merge two partial stacks of the same type | Mid-level, when you're tracking too many piles |
| 4 | Move into an empty slot to free a stack | Only when priorities 1–3 are unavailable |
| 5 | Move a capybara back where it came from | Never — that's an undo in disguise |

A few habits support that ranking:

- **Think in stacks, not in capybaras.** Ask "which stack do I want to finish next?" rather than "which capybara can I move?"
- **Keep one empty slot sacred.** In most builds, one reserved slot is worth more than two used ones.
- **Batch your colors.** Finishing a type completely removes it from your mental load.
- **Pause before the last three moves.** Endgames are where one careless empty-slot move costs the level.
- **Use undo to test, not to rescue.** If you're undoing more than a couple of times per level, the plan was wrong, not the execution.

## Five Beginner Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Community reports from sorting-game forums and player experience across capybara-themed releases consistently flag the same five errors.

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Empty-slot hoarding | Filling every empty slot "just in case" | Reserve at least one slot for emergencies |
| Chasing the top capybara | Always moving whatever is visible | Work backward from the buried type instead |
| Splitting stacks | Creating three half-finished piles of the same type | Merge before you start a new pile |
| Ignoring the counter | Burning moves early, panicking at the end | Track your move budget from move one |
| Undo spamming | Randomly reversing moves until something works | Undo once, rethink, then commit to a new plan |

The splitting-stacks mistake deserves a special mention. New players treat each stack as an independent problem, so they start "making progress" on four types at once. Experienced solvers finish one type completely, then the next. Fewer open piles means fewer constraints to hold in your head, and that mental bandwidth is what actually solves the level.

The move-counter mistake is subtler. If a level gives you a limited number of moves, the first third should be spent almost entirely on matching moves. Save the creative slot-juggling for the final third, when you can see exactly what's left.

## A Practice Plan That Actually Builds Skill

You don't need to grind. Twenty focused minutes beats two distracted hours. Here's a routine that works whether you're on level 3 or level 300.

| Session focus | What to practice | How you know it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Counting | Full board inventory before the first tap | You can name the most buried type instantly |
| Priority order | Never use an empty slot until priorities 1–3 are gone | Fewer undos per level |
| Endgame discipline | Slow down for the last five moves | Fewer one-move-from-winning losses |
| No-undo runs | Replay an easy level without undo | Better forward planning, not just faster recovery |
| Teach-back | Explain your solve out loud | You catch the logic gaps you were guessing through |

Two more things help more than they sound like they should. First, **take breaks**. Sorting puzzles punish tunnel vision, and a five-minute pause frequently reveals the move you stared past for ten minutes. Second, **replay early levels deliberately**. Easy boards are where you can build the counting habit without pressure, and the habit is what carries you through the harder ones.

Difficulty in these games usually ramps by adding types and reducing empty slots rather than by adding new mechanics — a pattern that community reports describe as consistent across most capybara sorters. That means your early-level habits scale directly. There's no new system to learn at level 80; there's just less room for sloppy thinking.

## FAQ

**Is sort the capybaras hard for a complete beginner?**
No. The first several levels are effectively tutorials, and the rules take about a minute to learn. The difficulty curve is gradual, so a beginner can usually clear early boards on the first or second attempt just by matching visible pairs. The real challenge starts when empty slots become scarce.

**Do I need to memorize anything to get good?**
Not really. You need two habits: counting the board before you move, and treating empty slots as a limited currency. Players who do those two things consistently solve boards that stumped them a week earlier.

**What should I do when I'm completely stuck?**
Work backward. Identify which stack you want to finish, then ask what the last three moves before that finish would have to be. Usually one of those moves is still available and you simply overlooked it because you were scanning forward.

**Can I finish levels without using undo at all?**
Yes, and it's a great training goal. Undo teaches recovery; playing without it teaches planning. Alternate between the two — use undo freely on hard levels, then replay an easier one with a self-imposed no-undo rule to sharpen your forward thinking.
